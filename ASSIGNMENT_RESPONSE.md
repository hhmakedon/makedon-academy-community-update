# Discussion response: Using AI to tell the data story

AI can support the way I shape and share Makedon Academy’s data story, but it should remain a drafting partner rather than the voice of the school. I can use an AI tool to identify patterns across de-identified information, test whether a message is understandable to families, suggest accessible headings, or create alternate versions for different communication channels. Those uses could make the story clearer and help me reach more community members without losing the central message.

The U.S. Department of Education (2023) emphasizes keeping people at the center, maintaining human judgment, advancing equity, and being transparent about AI use. Those principles are especially important when communicating about belonging and wellbeing. Before accepting AI-generated language or a visual “brand,” I would ask: Does this sound like me? Is the claim supported by our actual data? Whose experience is missing? Could this wording label or stereotype a group? Does it protect student privacy? A polished product is not appropriate if it hides uncertainty or makes a conclusion appear stronger than the evidence.

For this communication, AI could help organize the page, improve readability, and compare tone across drafts. I would still choose the focus, verify every claim, remove student-identifying information, and revise the final message in my own voice. I would also disclose meaningful AI assistance. Used this way, AI can strengthen clarity and consistency while the leader remains accountable for the story, the decisions, and the relationship with the community.

**Reference**

U.S. Department of Education, Office of Educational Technology. (2023). *Artificial intelligence and the future of teaching and learning: Insights and recommendations*. https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/documents/ai-report/ai-report.pdf
